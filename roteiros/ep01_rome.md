## 0:35 — #12: Fettuccine Alfredo is basically an American thing

`[CENA] Prato de fettuccine bordado. Uma bandeira dos EUA e uma da Itália costuradas lado a lado.`

Let's start with your dinner.
Fettuccine Alfredo was invented in Rome, by a guy named Alfredo di Lelio, in the early 1900s. He made it for his wife after she had a baby: just pasta, butter and cheese.
Then two Hollywood stars, Mary Pickford and Douglas Fairbanks, ate it on their honeymoon in 1920, loved it, and took the story back to America.
And that's where it blew up. Today, if you order "Fettuccine Alfredo" in most of Rome... a lot of waiters will just give you a look.

`[CENA] Garçom bordado com uma sobrancelha levantada. Gus imita a mesma cara.`

---

## 1:20 — #11: Caesar salad has nothing to do with Caesar

`[CENA] Busto de Júlio César bordado, segurando uma alface. Um "X" vermelho em ponto cruz aparece em cima.`

While we're at it: Caesar salad?
Not Julius. Not Rome. Not even Italy.
It was created in Tijuana, Mexico, in the 1920s, by an Italian immigrant and restaurant owner named Caesar Cardini.
Julius Caesar never had a single crouton. Poor guy.

## 1:55 — #10: The Spanish Steps were paid for by the French

`[CENA] Escadaria bordada, degrau por degrau sendo costurado de baixo para cima.`

The Spanish Steps. Famous. Beautiful. Very... Spanish?
Nope. They were mostly paid for with money left by a French diplomat, and finished in the 1720s. They're only called "Spanish" because the Spanish embassy sits right at the bottom.
And here's the part tourists learn the hard way: you're not allowed to sit on them anymore. Since 2019, sitting on the steps can get you a fine.

`[CENA] Turista bordado sentando; um apito; ele levanta num pulo. Gus ri.`

I sit on them all the time. Nobody fines a pigeon.

---

## 2:45 — #9: Rome's drinking fountains have a secret trick

`[CENA] Close de um "nasone": fonte de ferro com bico curvo. Gotas em ponto atrás.`

Rome has around two thousand five hundred little iron drinking fountains on the streets. Romans call them *nasoni*, "big noses", because of the curved spout.
The water is free, cold and drinkable.
But here's the trick: block the bottom of the spout with your finger, and the water shoots up out of a small hole on top, like a water fountain.

`[CENA] Dedo bordado tampa o bico; jato sobe em arco; Gus toma banho sem querer, sacode as penas.`

---

## 3:25 — #8: The Pantheon's dome has a hole... and it rains inside

`[CENA] Vista de baixo do domo do Panteão, quadrados (caixotões) costurados em espiral. Raio de luz amarelo pelo óculo.`

The Pantheon is almost nineteen hundred years old. And its dome is still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. No steel. Nothing. Just Roman concrete.
At the very top there's an open hole, about nine meters wide, called the oculus.
So yes, when it rains in Rome... it rains inside the Pantheon. The floor is slightly curved, with little drain holes, so the water just disappears.

`[CENA] Gotas caindo pelo óculo; Gus abre um guarda-chuva minúsculo.`

---

## 4:10 — #7: The ancient Romans built a sewer that still works

`[CENA] Corte lateral da cidade, como um mapa bordado: ruas em cima, túnel de pedra embaixo.`

Under your feet there's the Cloaca Maxima, one of the oldest sewer systems in the world. Parts of it go back around twenty-six centuries.
And some of it still carries water today.
Twenty-six centuries. Some apartments in New York can't keep the plumbing working for twenty-six days.

---

## 4:45 — #6: An emperor taxed pee. Seriously.

`[CENA] Imperador Vespasiano bordado, sério, com uma moeda na mão. Um balde ao fundo.`

Okay, this one's gross. Stay with me.
Ancient Romans collected urine. It was used by laundries and leather workers, because it helped clean clothes.
So Emperor Vespasian did what any government would do: he taxed it.
When his son complained, Vespasian held up a coin and said: "Pecunia non olet." *Money doesn't stink.*
And to this day, public urinals in Italy are still called *vespasiani*.

`[CENA] Gus tampa o bico com a asa, cara de nojo.`

---

## 5:35 — #5: There's an entire country inside Rome

`[CENA] Mapa de Roma bordado; uma linha de pesponto contorna o Vaticano; "zoom" no contorno.`

Inside the city of Rome sits a whole other country: Vatican City. It's the smallest country in the world, about 110 acres. You can walk across it in twenty minutes.
It has its own post office, its own passports... and an ATM where you can choose the instructions in Latin.

`[CENA] Caixa eletrônico bordado com a tela em latim. Gus aperta os botões, confuso.`

---

## 6:15 — #4: The Colosseum might have been flooded for sea battles

`[CENA] Coliseu bordado, linhas azuis de água subindo dentro da arena, barquinhos de feltro.`

You know the Colosseum for gladiators. But ancient writers claim that in its early years, the arena was flooded to stage mock naval battles, with real ships.
Historians still argue about how often, or how, it happened. Later, the Romans built a maze of tunnels under the floor, so flooding it would have been impossible.
But imagine buying a ticket and getting a *sea battle* in the middle of the city.

---

## 7:00 — #3: There's a hill in Rome made of broken pots

`[CENA] Colina bordada; zoom lento revela que é feita de cacos de ânforas costurados um a um.`

In the Testaccio neighborhood, there's a hill about 35 meters tall. Monte Testaccio.
It isn't made of dirt. It's made of broken pottery: an estimated fifty-three million ancient jars, called amphorae, used to ship olive oil into Rome.
The Romans couldn't reuse them, so they stacked them, very neatly, for centuries.
Ancient Rome's trash pile... turned into a landmark.

---

## 7:45 — #2: The coins in the Trevi Fountain feed people

`[CENA] Fontana di Trevi bordada; moedas em ponto dourado caem uma a uma.`

The legend says: throw one coin into the Trevi Fountain, and you'll come back to Rome.
And people *do*. About three thousand euros land in that fountain every single day. More than a million euros a year.
The city collects it, and the money goes to Caritas, a charity that uses it to help people in need, including meals.
So your wish actually does something.

`[CENA] Gus joga uma moedinha, fecha os olhos, faz um pedido.`

---

## 8:30 — #1: Rome's cats live where Julius Caesar was murdered

`[CENA] Música para. Silêncio de 1 segundo. Ruínas bordadas no centro da cidade, com colunas quebradas.`

And now... number one.
In the middle of modern Rome there's a sunken square full of ancient ruins: Largo di Torre Argentina.
This is the area where, in 44 BC, Julius Caesar was stabbed to death by the senators.
And today? It's a cat sanctuary.
Dozens of cats live in the ruins, sleeping on two-thousand-year-old stones, cared for by volunteers. Rome even protects its street cats as part of the city's "bio-cultural heritage."

`[CENA] Gatos bordados aparecem um a um nas colunas. Um deles encara o Gus.`

The most famous murder scene in history... is now a nap spot.
And the cats there? They do *not* like pigeons.

`[CENA] Gus sai voando apressado, deixando uma pena para trás.`
